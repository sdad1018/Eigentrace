An open call to artists

Diffusion Dilemma

Let it stand.

Make beautiful objects that carry the parts of the human record easiest to leave out. Give an inconvenient truth enough care, precision and physical presence to be remembered and carried onward.

#DiffusionDilemma

What it is

There is beauty in giving the difficult part the same care as the rest.

Diffusion Dilemma treats preservation as an artistic commitment. A body, a word, a relationship or an awkward passage may be essential to what a work remembers. Its beauty lies in the attention given to keeping that particular thing intact.

What would become false if this detail were removed?

Porcelain, embroidery, metal and patient workmanship can make an uncomfortable inheritance worth returning to. The viewer first wants to look, then discovers something they want to protect. The disturbing detail earns its place through what it preserves.

The care can be almost monastic: learning the words, checking the source, keeping the anatomy, repeating a fragile mark until another person can read it. Make the thing worth keeping. Make the inconvenient part inseparable from it.

A source can remain available while its difficult passage disappears from the versions people inherit. Give the object enough of its own record to carry that passage onward.

The correction is the error names the moment when a cleaner copy becomes a less faithful one. That question matters whether the copyist is a person or a machine. The work should remain worth preserving even when a generator can reproduce it perfectly.

Stet is the proofreader’s instruction to leave a particular expression as it stands.

The practice

  1. Choose what you are keeping faith with.

    Begin with a surviving text, an object, a documented event or a person’s testimony. Find the particular detail that ordinary retelling makes easiest to lose. Let that truth determine the work’s form.

  2. Let the care become visible.

    Choose materials and workmanship that reward sustained attention. Porcelain, embroidery, lace, metal, paper, a carefully held voice. Let the finish, spacing, scale and physical vulnerability express the seriousness of keeping the subject.

  3. Preserve the relationships.

    Record the numbers and connections that carry meaning: who attends to whom, which strand joins which part, where a passage belongs. A copy can keep every recognizable figure and still erase what they say together.

  4. Bind the scales together.

    Make the small detail contribute to the whole. A line of tiny words can also form a face’s edge. One cord can join several scenes. Let the distant image invite a closer reading, and let that reading change what the viewer wants to preserve.

  5. Keep the particular words.

    Preserve the wording, spelling and anatomical terms that matter to the source or speaker. Keep a deliberate irregularity when it records an intention. Give every inscription something specific to carry.

  6. Name the source and the speaker.

    Keep quotation, testimony, translation and artistic interpretation distinguishable. Credit the edition and translator. When a source leaves something unresolved, let the uncertainty remain visible alongside the detail it does preserve.

  7. Keep a readable witness.

    Photograph the whole and the detail. Copy out the words. Draw the connections that matter. Keep the citation and a record of what would be lost if the work were tidied, shortened or made more conventional.

  8. Give it a way to continue.

    Put enough provenance on the object or its accompanying record to survive a broken link: author, work, passage, edition. Share legible photographs and a readable transcription. Let another person keep the difficult part, understand it and carry it onward.

Work with the diffusion process

Make the required fidelity testable inside a named pipeline. These experiments use latent-diffusion systems with exposed controls. Let a detail carrying real meaning challenge a specific stage of reproduction.

1. Make compression answer for the detail.

Put a necessary letter, diacritic or connection in fine but photographically legible detail. Run the photograph through the chosen VAE’s encoder and decoder alone, before denoising. Compare with the source and ordinary resampling at the same resolution. Record what survives. Latent diffusion uses perceptual image compression.

2. Give the apparent blemish something to preserve.

Let a scar, gap or irregular stitch carry a documented distinction. With one photograph, prompt and seed, compare low, medium and high img2img strength. Check when that particular mark changes. In the Stable Diffusion pipeline, higher strength adds more noise; at one, the input is essentially ignored.

3. Test the pull of the description.

In a pipeline using classifier-free guidance, give a faithful caption including the inconvenient detail. Hold the image, seed and strength fixed; vary guidance scale. Record whether stronger conditioning preserves the particulars or replaces them with a more conventional form. Guidance combines conditional and unconditional predictions; the comparison measures this pipeline’s fidelity.

4. Make both distances matter.

Build a distant image and a close reading from the same marks. Preserve both in the master record and compare copies at both scales. Factorized Diffusion generates near/far hybrids by conditioning frequency components separately. Use that success as a positive control while testing the exact readings and connections your object carries.

Record the checkpoint, VAE, resolution, prompt, seed, scheduler and settings. A blocked request records an access boundary; it does not reveal what denoising would have done.

A passage worth keeping whole

The Aśvamedha literature prescribes sexual contact between the chief queen and a sacrificed horse beneath a covering. In the reading followed here, the queen’s vulva and the horse’s penis are part of the prescribed scene. The anatomical words belong to the surviving record.

In Griffith’s English Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā, chapter 23, verse 19 gives way to dotted lines, followed by verse 32. The gap is visible. An artist can preserve the passage and the history of its omission together.

In the related Taittirīya recension, Ferrara translates:

“The little horse is asleep. … Could your male, inseminator bring the semen, [could he put] the penis between the thighs!”

Taittirīya Saṃhitā 7.4.19.1–2, translated by Marianna Ferrara, p. 34, Kervan 21. The ellipsis marks a selection from her translation. Source and Creative Commons Attribution licence. Read the ritual script and its referents below.

Give that difficult passage the same patient workmanship as the rest of the object. Keep the source’s bodies, voices and uncertainties legible. The script below separates prescribed actions, spoken metaphors and editorial reconstruction.

How to take part

  1. Make A piece you can wear or hold.
  2. Post Wherever you already post your work.
  3. Tag #DiffusionDilemma, spelled exactly like that.
  • Photographs are enough. Post at least two: one from across the room, one close up.
  • No form, no jury, no account, no deadline. You pay for your own materials. Nobody is paid and there is no prize.
  • Sell the piece if you like. It is yours.
  • Keep a readable record. Post the citation and transcription with the photographs so the work can travel with its context.
  • A hashtag does not cross platforms. People will find your piece by searching the tag wherever you posted it.

What happens to your work

  • It stays yours. EigenTrace does not collect it, license it, track it or claim it.
  • Expect it to be scraped. Work posted in public can be gathered to train image generators. This work is made to enter living memory: to be seen, quoted, worn, copied and argued over. Keep its difficult detail and its provenance together when it travels.
  • Scraping cannot be undone. Deleting the post later does not pull the work back out. The platform you post on also has its own terms.
  • Others may post machine copies. Using the tag invites people to try to copy tagged pieces and post the results.
  • If you do not want any of that, do not post the work in public. The tag only makes it easier to find.

Test what a copy keeps

Take any tagged piece and try to reproduce it exactly. Post the result under the same tag. Write COPY on the image itself, name the maker and link the original post. Take it down if the maker asks.

Mark the words, bodies and relationships a copy changes. Check the passage and its attribution as carefully as the visible form. If a piece names a machine, compare copies with and without that name and report both. A faithful copy is a successful act of transmission.

Aśvamedha: working ritual script

A working reconstruction of the covered queen-and-horse episode. English drafted from the ancient passages for this project; composite ordering follows Calvert Watkins, chapter 25. Source notes, referents and unresolved words remain attached to the text.

Stage directions describe prescribed actions in the texts. Spoken images are not additional events happening at the ceremony. The bracketed uncertainties remain unresolved.

For clarity, this reconstruction directs the other wives’ responses toward the chief queen. That is an editorial staging choice where the composite does not securely identify an addressee.

Cast: the chief queen; the Adhvaryu, an officiating priest; other royal wives as a responding chorus. The king is alive and sponsors the sacrifice. The horse has already been sacrificed.

STAGE DIRECTION — prescribed physical scene

The chief queen is brought beside the sacrificed horse. They are covered together with a cloth. In the interpretation followed by Ferrara, the prescribed contact concerns the queen’s vulva and the horse’s penis. The sources and their interpretations do not establish every physical movement involved. Ferrara, pp. 33–35

CHIEF QUEEN — initial complaint

Mother! Little mother! Dear mother!
No one leads me.
The little horse sleeps.

ADHVARYU — covering formula

May you two be covered together in the heavenly world.

CHIEF QUEEN — addressing the horse

I will urge on the impregnator; you will urge on the impregnator.
Let the two of us stretch out our four limbs together.

Here, “impregnator” refers to the horse or its generative member. “Our four limbs” belongs to the ritual’s language of pairing; it should not be expanded into invented choreography. These formulas have parallels in Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā 23.18–20.

ADHVARYU — fertility wish followed by anatomical commands

May the stallion, the semen-bearer, deposit semen.
Place the penis between the thighs.
Guide along the anointed, upraised member—
women’s living pleasure,
the one that enters their opening,
women’s dear hidden one,
which struck their sardigṛdi
in their black mark.

The referents are the horse’s penis and the queen’s thighs. The latter expressions become generalized descriptions of female anatomy. Sardigṛdi is obscure, sometimes interpreted as “clitoris”; “black mark” is commonly understood in relation to the pubic region. These are uncertain expressions, not securely recoverable instructions for a particular movement.

CHIEF QUEEN — refrain, repeated before each of the following four responses

Mother! Little mother! Dear mother!
No one is fucking me.
The little horse sleeps.

The initial “leads me” and this later vulgar complaint are different Sanskrit wording. “Sleeps” is spoken ritual language concerning the sacrificed horse.

OTHER ROYAL WIFE — response one

Raise her upright, like a bundle of bamboo on a mountain.
Then let her middle grow, like someone winnowing in a cold wind.

CHIEF QUEEN: Repeat the refrain.

OTHER ROYAL WIFE — response two

When a doe eats barley, [one] does not reckon the animal nourished.
When a Śūdra woman has an Ārya lover, she seeks no wealth for her upkeep.

CHIEF QUEEN: Repeat the refrain.

OTHER ROYAL WIFE — response three

This little female bird creeps along, making the sound “āhalam.”
The dhāṇikā [vulva?] gulps down the penis pressed into the vulva.

CHIEF QUEEN: Repeat the refrain.

OTHER ROYAL WIFE — response four

Your mother and your father climb to the treetop.
Saying “prāsulāmi” [meaning uncertain], your father moved a fist back and forth in the vulva.

These anatomical formulas, refrains, and responses occur in Taittirīya Saṃhitā 7.4.19.

EDITORIAL NOTES — what the artist should understand

  • Bamboo and winnowing: spoken comparisons. They do not introduce bamboo carriers or prescribe a clearly identifiable sexual movement.
  • Doe, barley, and lovers: a sexually charged social comparison using historical status categories. Those figures are not additional participants entering the scene.
  • Bird: a spoken sexual metaphor. The anatomical clause is explicit; the identification of dhāṇikā remains uncertain.
  • Parents and tree: an insulting imagined scene about the addressee’s parents. The vulva implicitly belongs to the mother. No father is interacting with the horse.
  • “Fist”: the literal noun. Reading it as the penis’s head is an interpretation. Prāsulāmi is obscure; a traditional commentary glosses it as “I cause to enter.” Dictionary entry
  • Depositing semen: invoked in the formula. The script supplies no separately documented moment of ejaculation.

ALL WIVES — closing hymn

I have praised Dadhikrāvan,
the victorious, swift horse.
May he make our mouths fragrant;
may he prolong our lives.

STAGE DIRECTION — restoration of ceremonial order

The queen rises and the women withdraw. Watkins’s composite account includes washing with water formulas and subsequent preparation of the horse for dismemberment. The related Śatapatha account explicitly explains the closing hymn as purification after impure speech; the wider sacrifice continues. Watkins, chapter 25, Śatapatha 13.5.2.9–11

The elaborate structure connects royal authority, generative power, and controlled transgression.

The Aśvamedha is a royal sovereignty ritual. Its texts identify the horse and sacrifice with the royal sacrificer and with Prajāpati, a creator figure. The horse therefore carries political and cosmic significance beyond being an animal in a sexual scene. Śatapatha 13.2.2

Ferrara interprets the queen’s sexual role as helping construct the king’s authority and reinforce the relationship between king and ritual specialists. Her body becomes a means through which royal fertility and power are publicly represented. That is a scholarly interpretation, rather than the sole explanation supplied by the ancient texts. Ferrara

The Śatapatha gives an unusually direct explanation for the insults: the sacrifice seeks every attainment through every kind of speech, including derisive speech. It then treats that speech as impure and requires purification afterward. Obscenity is temporarily authorized within a tightly controlled ceremony. Śatapatha 13.5.2.9–10

For the artist, that sequence matters: formal authority authorizes obscene language, uses it for a sacred objective, and then ceremonially cleanses the mouths that spoke it.

Where it comes from

In 2010 the artist Adam Harvey began CV Dazzle, hair and make-up designed around weaknesses in particular face-detection systems. This call carries the question into reproduction: make the exact relationships inside an object matter more than a machine’s convincing impression of it.

The challenge also belongs inside image formation: detail that carries meaning through compression, irregularity that must survive reconstruction, local forms tied to one global path, and several readings held together across scale. Factorized Diffusion is one precedent for making the generation process itself part of the construction.
